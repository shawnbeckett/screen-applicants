# Deriving axes and writing assessments

## Choosing the axes

Three or four, taken from what the posting actually asks for. Each must be
something a resume can evidence and that candidates will genuinely differ on.

Good axes separate the field. If everyone scores 4, the axis is measuring
nothing. If it cannot be evidenced from an application, it belongs in the
interview, not here.

Worked example, from a PR Account Coordinator posting that asked for agency
internship experience, excellent writing, and curiosity about B2B technology:

| Axis | 1 | 3 | 5 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Agency depth | No agency exposure | An agency internship, or a coordinator title elsewhere | Years in an agency account seat |
| Writing | No evidence beyond the application | Some professional writing, no published work | Published bylines or press materials that ran |
| B2B tech affinity | No sign of interest | Adjacent sector, or stated interest | Worked the posting's actual sectors |

For a different role the axes change completely. A developer posting might use
shipped work, language depth, and systems reasoning. Never carry axes across
roles.

## Scoring

Score every candidate in one pass, in one context. Hold the whole field in view
so a 4 means the same thing at the top and the bottom of the list.

Anchor the ends first. Find the clearest 5 and the clearest 1 on each axis, then
place everyone else between them. Use the full range. A run where nobody scores
1 or 5 is a run where the axis was not discriminating.

Score what the application evidences, not what it claims to be. "Strong
communicator" is not evidence. A published article is.

## Tiers

Tiers are a judgment, not a formula. Total score is an input, not the answer. A
candidate can carry a high total and still belong lower because of something the
axes do not capture: a stale gap, a sector mismatch, seniority above the band.

Say the reason in the `watch` field. Every tier placement should be defensible
in one sentence to someone who disagrees.

## Writing the assessment

`assessment` is 30 to 70 words on why this person is where they are. Lead with
the strongest concrete fact. Name employers, publications, tools and numbers
from their own documents.

`watch` is one sentence naming the single thing that would most likely make this
hire go wrong. Every candidate has one. If nothing comes to mind, look harder.

`decide_first` is rare. Use it when something must be settled before the
candidate is contacted at all, such as compensation being clearly above band.

Write plainly. State the fact and let it carry. Avoid stacking adjectives.

### Comparative claims

Claims like "the strongest agency profile here" are useful precisely because one
reader saw the whole field. They are also the first thing to become false if the
pool changes, so make them only when the comparison actually holds across
everyone scored in this run.

## Honesty about the output

Every number on a resume is the applicant's own assertion. Quote them as claims,
never as verified fact. Scores are a model's judgment, consistent within a run
and not comparable across runs or against another reviewer. Say so when handing
over the board.
